## Final project: U.S. employment patterns

The project lets an audience compare how employment patterns change across age groups, education levels, and gender. It turns **44 annual rows labelled 1979–2022** into a seven-worksheet Excel analysis, a **22-slide presentation**, and **bilingual speaking notes**. Viewers can follow overall trends, selected periods, and differences between groups without reading the original table row by row.

The age section compares four age groups. The education section compares five categories and calculates the advanced-degree minus bachelor’s-degree gap. The gender section compares men and women, including differences within age groups. Line charts show change over time, bar charts support group comparisons, and a separate difference chart makes the size of a gap easier to inspect.

The original presentation credits **Shaoxuan Xiong as producer** and **Richard Feng as editor**. The speaking notes retain the speakers’ section labels and rehearsal annotations.

## What I practiced during the course

I practiced organizing numeric and categorical data, calculating counts, averages, standard deviations, and frequencies, and choosing a chart for the question. Titanic and coin-mass exercises developed those skills on individual records and measurements before the final project applied them to annual subgroup comparisons.

I also practiced making comparisons more explicit through differences and proportions. That includes checking what a percentage uses as its denominator: the share of survivors who travelled first class is a different calculation from the survival rate among first-class passengers. The chart-comparison exercise gave me a way to compare how different displays emphasize totals or component stages.

The final project connected those spreadsheet skills with communication. Bilingual rehearsal notes and a collaborative presentation involved dividing speaking sections, connecting the parts of the talk, and explaining groups, time ranges, and changes in English.
